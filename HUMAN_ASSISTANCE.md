# What a person is needed for

Round 3 ran everything an agent can run alone (see report/src/collection_round3.md). The items below need a person: a browser with ordinary access, a subscription, a judgement the model should not make, or a second pair of eyes. They are ordered by how much they would change the results.

## 1. Download 74 instruments whose official hosts refuse the research crawler

The collectors identify themselves honestly (`CEI-AI-Robotics-collector/1.0`) and do not imitate a browser or bypass bot checks, so some official legal databases return nothing to them. A person with an ordinary browser can save each document (the HTML page or the PDF) into `/home/user/collection-round-3/texts/<candidate_id>.txt` (plain text) or `raw/<candidate_id>.pdf`; the fetch and annotation scripts then pick them up (`scripts/fetch_texts_r3.py` skips ids that already have a text). Each file needs the source URL on its first line as `SOURCE_URL: ...`.

## 4. Hand-label the 200-document validation sample (about two working days)

No person has yet labelled a document in this study; the precision audits are model-on-model. The kit in `data/human_validation/` has a stratified sample of 200 documents (40 per stratum), a coding sheet with drop-downs (`coding_sheet.xlsx`), instructions, and an answer key of the model's labels kept separate so the coder is blind to them. A coder with legal or policy training fills the sheet from the stored text (`texts/<doc_id>.txt`) or the source URL; `scripts/22_human_agreement.py` (to be written against the returned sheet, following `21_precision_audit.py`) then reports agreement per field and recalibrates the three headline counts. Languages in the sample: English 157, Japanese 12, Chinese 8, Portuguese 6, Spanish 4, French 4; a second coder for the non-English documents is useful.

## 5. Check the model's legal readings where it was unsure

The supplementary pass (whom the rule binds, dates, legal effect, provision dating) rated its own confidence. A lawyer should check the 57 principal instruments where the model's confidence is below 0.6, the 15 standards whose legal effect could not be determined from the preview, the 7 binding instruments whose robot provision the model dated later than the document year (Table 73), and above all the 54 binding instruments (including 15 consolidated statutes dated before 2010) for which the captured text carries no amendment history, so the year the robot provision arrived could not be read: these dates drive the provision-level trend and a wrong amendment year moves a document between periods. The ids are in `data/ids_legal_check.txt`; the quotes the model relied on are in `annotations/supplement_v5/<doc_id>.json`.

## 6. Decide the 27 audited documents the second audit would exclude

The second precision audit (review/v5_audit/precision_audit_v5.md) lists the sampled principal binding instruments that failed one of the three main criteria (distinct instrument, binding, substantive embodied provisions). Whether to exclude them, relabel them (for instance as amendments or as general-AI instruments) or change the criteria is an editorial decision. Accepting the audit's reading and extrapolating it is what the report does now; a person's ruling on each of the failing documents would let the counts be corrected directly rather than by a sample-based factor. Three of the failure types are also fixable by a person with a browser: the register, table-of-contents and bill-tracking captures (the Australian CASR 1998 and its Part 101 manual, the Kentucky and Utah bills, the Nigerian Part 21 entry) need the operative text saved as in item 1; the UK retained copies of EU drone regulations need a ruling on whether a retained copy is a distinct instrument; and the generic regimes rated relevant (the Digital Omnibus on AI, UN R155 and R156, the medical-device regulations) need a ruling on whether a regime that governs robots only through general product rules belongs in the count.

## 7. Boundary decisions only the authors can make

- Whether consolidated statutes amended for robots (the Japanese Road Traffic Act and Road Transport Vehicle Act, the German Road Traffic Act, the US Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards) count as one instrument each or as one per amendment; the counts in the report treat them as one and date them by the provision year where the text gives it (Table 73).
- Whether purely sectoral vehicle and drone rules belong in the 'embodied AI' set at all, or only those that mention automation, autonomy or AI. The report gives both (Table 53b counts principal instruments with and without autonomy or AI provisions); the headline needs one.
- The reference list of 'instruments every collection should have' (17 titles) was compiled by the agent; an expert panel should settle it before recall is reported against it.
- Which categories in the governance-gap index count as governed by general product-safety law rather than by a robot-specific instrument (the index treats both as instruments; a stricter reading changes the gap for care, delivery and humanoid robots most).
