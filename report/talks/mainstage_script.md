# Who governs the robots? Main-stage script (TED-style, 15 slides)

Written for a main-stage talk of about thirteen minutes. One speaker, no notes, slides as backdrop. Each heading names the slide; the paragraph beneath it is spoken while that slide is on screen. About 150 words a minute. Numbers are from version 5 of the study (November 2026).

## 1. Title

You have seen the videos. A robot with two arms and two legs folds a towel, badly, then well. Another one walks across a warehouse carrying a box. Another sits by a bed in a care home in Japan and purrs when you stroke it. Every one of those videos ends the same way, with the same question in the comments: who is in charge of this thing? I spent the last year trying to answer that question with data instead of opinions. Not who should be in charge. Who is. Tonight I want to show you what the rules of the world actually say about machines that think and move. The short version is: less than you think, about different robots than you think, and the gap is in the one place we are about to put them.

## 2. Scene

Somewhere, right now, a robot is being built to live in your mother's room. Not a factory arm behind a fence with a yellow line on the floor. A machine that lifts her when she cannot lift herself. That carries things. That reminds her to take the pills. That watches her at night, and talks back when she talks to it. Every company in this field is building toward that room, because that is where the money is and, honestly, because that is where the need is. So here is the question I could not stop asking. When that machine is in that room, what rules will it be following? Whose rules? Written when, and by whom?

## 3. We went looking

So we went looking for the rules. All of them. Laws, regulations, standards, government strategies, scientific papers, news. Three thousand three hundred and twenty documents, from a hundred and nineteen countries, the European Union and the United Nations system. And I have to tell you something about how we read them, because it matters. No human read them all. Nobody could. An AI did. It asked every single document the same thirty-nine questions. Is this binding? What machine is it about? What does it say about safety, about who is in control, about who pays? Then other AIs checked its answers, and an independent one audited them. I will come back to how much you should trust that. Keep it in mind. For now, here is what the map shows.

## 4. 278

Two hundred and seventy-eight. That is how many binding rules there are on Earth that reach a robot. Binding means laws with teeth: break them and something happens to you. A fine, a ban, a prison sentence. We counted each one once, with its amendments but without its paperwork, because legal databases will hand you the same law as twelve separate entries if you let them. Two hundred and seventy-eight. For every robot, every drone, every self-driving car, every machine that moves on its own, in every country we could reach. That number is a floor; we certainly missed some. But it is not a thousand, and it is not ten thousand.

## 5. 25

And of those two hundred and seventy-eight, twenty-five mention artificial intelligence. Twenty-five. The other two hundred and fifty-three were written for something else. For aircraft. For vehicles. For machinery. For consumer products. For medical devices. They reach robots by accident of definition. Robots, it turns out, are governed sideways.

## 6. Governed sideways

Here is what sideways looks like. A hundred and forty-six of the rules are about drones. Why? Because a drone is, legally, an aircraft, and we have had aviation law for a century. A hundred and nine are about self-driving cars. Because a self-driving car is, legally, a vehicle, and every country has a road-traffic act. Thirteen are about factory robot arms. Because an arm is, legally, a machine, and machinery law has been protecting workers since the steam engine. None of those laws began with someone asking, what should a thinking machine be allowed to do? They began with a category. The robot fell into it.

## 7. And the robots we will live with?

So what about the robots that do not fall into an old category? The robot in your mother's room. The companion. The humanoid. We counted. Binding rules for companion robots: zero. For humanoids: zero. For care robots: four, and all four are laws about something else, a robotics industry act here, a special economic zone there, that mention care robots in passing. Four hundred and twenty-nine documents in our collection are about these three kinds of machine. Four of them have legal force. The one text in the world written specifically for care robots is a voluntary engineering standard from 2014. That is the entire legal inheritance of the machine that will help your mother out of bed.

## 8. Companion robots

Now here is the part that surprised me most. We also had five hundred and nine scientific papers in the collection, and we asked which robots they were about. Forty-three percent of the research on robot governance is about companion and social robots. Three percent of the rules are. Scientists are studying the robots we will live with: how people bond with them, whether they deceive, what happens when a lonely person depends on one. Lawmakers are regulating the ones already in the street. The two communities are looking at different machines. We checked whether that was an artefact of where we searched. It is not. The gap is real, and it is widest exactly where the machines are heading.

## 9. After the accident

Why does it happen this way? Because rules follow machines into public space, and usually they follow the first accident. Look at the pattern. Drone rules came mostly between 2016 and 2021, after the near-misses at airports, after the first drone hit a passenger aircraft's wing. Self-driving rules came from 2017 on, after the test fleets were already on the road, and after the first deaths. Sidewalk delivery robot laws came between 2018 and 2022, after the robots were already on the pavement, bumping into people's ankles. Three quarters of all these rules were written in the last seven years. We regulate what has already hurt somebody. The robots in the videos have not hurt anybody yet. That is why they have no rules. And that is the window.

## 10. The body changes the question

Let me tell you why this is not just the AI debate with a different hat on. When software goes wrong you get a bad sentence or a bad score. When a machine with a body goes wrong you get an event. And so the questions change. Can it hurt you? A two-hundred-kilogram arm can. How close may it come? Who pays when it breaks something, or someone? And the hardest one: how much may it decide by itself before a person has to step in? We counted what the rules actually spell out. Physical safety, in detail, in a hundred and sixty-seven of the two hundred and seventy-eight. Fairness, in three. Human dignity, in one. The AI ethics conversation of the last decade, bias, transparency, dignity, is almost absent from the rulebook for machines with bodies. The rulebook for a body is about the body.

## 11. Mention is not detail

And that hardest question, how much may it decide alone, is where the rules go quiet. A hundred and seventy-one of them raise it. They say there shall be appropriate human control. Seventy-three actually answer it: what is allowed, by whom, under what conditions, checked how. And almost all of those seventy-three are in aviation and road law, where remote pilot and fallback driver already mean something. Everywhere else, the law gestures and moves on. Mention is not detail. And the distance between mention and detail is where the lawsuits of the next ten years are going to live.

## 12. Who has to obey

One more thing we asked every rule: who, in your own words, has to obey you? Sixty-two percent name the companies that operate the machine. Thirty-nine percent name the companies that build it. Eighteen percent name you, the person in the room with it. And eight percent, one rule in twelve, name the people who wrote the software that does the deciding. The duty lands on whoever runs the machine in the world. The people who shape its judgement are almost nowhere in the law. If that strikes you as backwards, you are not alone.

## 13. AI read the law about AI

Now the promise I made. We used AI to read the laws about AI, and you should want to know how much to trust that. So we did something simple. We took a random hundred of those binding rules and gave them to a separate AI auditor with three strict tests: is this really a distinct law, is it really binding, does it really say something about a machine? Seventy-three passed. A year ago it was fifty-four. Seventy-three is good. Seventy-three is not a hundred. What failed is instructive: laws that mention a drone in a list of an agency's duties, index pages captured instead of the statute, the same European regulation counted twice. And the number of these documents a human has labelled, so far, is zero. This is a map drawn by machines, waiting for a person to walk it. Every number I have given you tonight is an honest estimate with an honest error bar. Please check them. The whole thing is public.

## 14. Three things

So what do we do with the window? Three things, before the robots arrive. One. Write the rules first this time. Companions, carers, humanoids: zero, four, zero. We can see them coming. We know what room they are headed for. Drones did not get that courtesy; they got their rules after the airport. Two. Answer the control question; do not just raise it. Who may let the machine decide what, under which conditions, checked by whom. Aviation did it. Everyone else can. Three. Make the safety knowledge public. The documents that say how close, how fast, how much force, are engineering standards, and fifty-seven of the sixty-one we found are behind a paywall. We could not read them, and neither can you. Rules you cannot read are rules you cannot follow.

## 15. Close

I started with the videos, and the question in the comments. Who is in charge of this thing? Here is the honest answer, from three thousand documents. For the machines in the street and the sky, a patchwork of old law, stretched. For the machines that are about to walk into our homes, almost nobody. Not yet. The question is not whether robots will come into our homes. They are coming. The strategies are written, the factories are tooling up, the demonstrations are on every feed. The question is whether the rules will be there when they do. Right now, the answer is no. And for once, we can see it coming. Thank you.
