---
title: "Who governs the robots? What 1,656 documents from eight world regions say"
subtitle: "A one-page brief (v2, after independent review)"
author: "Centre for Enterprise Innovation (CEI) AI Robotics project, with Claude Code"
date: "31 October 2026"
---

**In July 2025 El Salvador put into force a law that governs robots as robots.** Almost everywhere else, the rules that bind a humanoid, a surgical arm or a delivery robot were written for cars, factories, aircraft, medical devices or AI in general. That is the central finding of a study of 1,656 documents (laws, bills, strategies, standards, guidelines, government reports, research papers and news) collected by eight regional teams and read and labelled by an AI model. No human read every document; a second AI model agreed with the first on about nine labels in ten, and the labels should be read as consistent, not certified.

**The big numbers.** 675 of the usable documents are about robots or other "embodied AI" (AI in a body). Of those, 65 are rules or policies of any kind, 34 of them written for robots specifically, and 5 are legally binding. Four of the five are in force: El Salvador's Robotic Technologies Law (2025), Dubai's autonomous-vehicle law (2023), an Argentine resolution creating a security-AI unit (2024) and the EU AI Act (2024), which reaches robots through its lists of regulated products. The fifth, a 2023 United States executive order, was revoked in January 2025. These are counts *within this collection*: an audit against a reference list of 38 known instruments shows that several binding laws for self-driving cars, drones and factory robots (the UK Automated Vehicles Act, Germany's autonomous-driving law, Japan's Level-4 rules, the EU Machinery Regulation, Korea's 2008 Intelligent Robots Act) were never captured, so the true number of binding rules is higher. What the collection does show is *how* binding law reaches robots: through sector law and general AI law, not robotics statutes.

**Scientists study companion robots. Lawmakers regulate cars.** Companion and social robots are the subject of 43% of the robot-governance research papers (219 of 508) but of 4 of the 65 robot rules (6%), none of them binding. Self-driving cars are 12% of research and 66% of rules (43 of 65); industrial robot arms 10% and 46%; drones 6% and 34%. The uncertainty intervals around these gaps exclude zero. Humanoid robots, the focus of 2025's industrial strategies, have 141 documents, 9 policy papers, one bill (Brazil, 2026: bans sales to minors and requires an emergency shutdown) and no binding law.

**Robot rules are machinery rules; AI rules are audit rules.** Among rules and policies, those about robots refer to technical standards (72% vs 36% for general AI), require testing or sandboxes (60% vs 33%) and conformity assessment, the pre-market safety check used for machinery (32% vs 12%). General-AI rules rely more on audits, labelling and data governance. Robot documents of every kind raise physical safety, human-robot proximity, liability for physical damage and the question of how much autonomy a machine may keep; after allowing for the fact that most robot documents are research papers, these differences remain large, while differences on the ethical core (fairness, transparency, accountability) mostly disappear.

**Everyone raises the control question; almost nobody has written it down.** "How much autonomy may a robot keep, and when must a human be in the loop?" is raised by 43 to 70% of robot rules of every type. It is set out in detail by one in five binding rules and by one in twenty voluntary guidelines in this collection. Technical standards set out physical safety in detail; they are also the texts that the policy literature cites least.

**Regions agree on values and differ on liability and control.** Transparency, fairness, avoiding harm and benefit are discussed at similar rates everywhere. What varies is liability for physical damage (from 8% of robot documents in the international standards layer to 63% in the Middle East), human oversight and autonomy-level control, and two tools: liability regimes and references to standards. Europe and North America are statistically indistinguishable in how they discuss robot governance; the Middle East, with a liability-led legal literature, and the standards layer stand apart. No country has more than five robot-specific instruments in the collection.

**What is changing.** Since 2025 the robot debate has shifted toward cybersecurity (the odds that a robot document addresses it have nearly tripled, after allowing for the changing mix of documents) and away from the robot-personhood debate; human oversight is rising at the edge of significance. The much-discussed turn to "industrial policy" is mostly a change in which documents are being written, not in what research says. Risk-based tiering and conformity assessment are appearing in robot documents about 30% more often each year. Within the robot literature, humanoids rose from 17% of documents in 2023 to 2024 to 29% in 2025 to 2026, and the terms "embodied AI" or "physical AI" from 2% of robot documents in 2024 to 23% in 2026 (to October).

**What it means for you.** A factory worker beside a collaborative robot: your protection is ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 and, in the EU, machinery law. Someone buying a companion robot for a parent: in this collection, the rules that reach it are data-protection law and a care-robot safety standard, not a robot law. A city buying delivery robots: nothing robot-specific; road and product law. A hospital buying a surgical robot: medical-device law and the AI Act's medical-device provisions.

**Read with care.** 43% of the documents are European and 79% English; only seven are in Chinese. The collection sought research on robots and official texts on AI in general, so the ratio of papers to rules is partly by design. Every label was assigned by an AI model (Claude Sonnet 5.5) and checked against a second model, a re-run, a full-text re-read and a version with the collectors' labels removed; it was not checked by human coders. 2026 runs to October. All counts of instruments are lower bounds. Version 2 of this study was produced after six independent adversarial reviews; the review log lists every finding and what changed.
