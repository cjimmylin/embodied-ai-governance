# Who governs the robots? Narration script for the expanded deck (v2, 41 slides)

Spoken by one narrator. Each heading names the slide; the paragraph beneath it is read while that slide is on screen. Target pace: about 150 words a minute. Total running time: about 28 minutes.

## 1. Cover

Who governs the robots? This is a study of three thousand one hundred and sixty-five documents from eight regions of the world: laws, standards, strategies and research papers about machines that act in the physical world. It was built, labelled and reviewed with the help of AI agents, and no person read every document. Over the next half hour, we will walk through what was collected, how it was read, what it says, and how far the numbers can be trusted. You do not need any background in AI or in law to follow along.

## 2. Why machines with bodies need their own look

Most writing about rules for AI is about software: chatbots, recommendation systems, credit scoring. A robot, a drone or a self-driving car is different. It is AI with a body. When it goes wrong, the harm is physical, and the questions change. Can it hurt someone? A two-hundred-kilogram arm or a one-and-a-half-tonne car can. How close may it work to people? Who pays when it damages property or injures a person? And how much may the machine decide on its own, before a person has to step in? The study calls this embodied AI, and asks what rules exist for it anywhere in the world, and what those rules actually say.

## 3. Two literatures that rarely met with numbers

Two bodies of work came before this one. The first is about AI ethics and AI law. Reviews of ethics guidelines found the same handful of principles everywhere, and little effect on practice, which pushed governments toward binding rules with risk tiers, testing before sale, audits and oversight bodies. All of that is about software. The second is robot law and robot ethics, which argues that machines acting physically raise their own questions: bodily harm, proximity, liability, autonomy and certification. That work is mostly expert argument, rarely counted. Existing databases list policies but do not separate machines with bodies or code what each text says. This study is the first coded collection that does, and the first to report how its own counts move with the counting unit and the search strategy.

## 4. The questions we asked

Six questions. First, what rules for robots exist, and how many? The answer, it turns out, changes with what you decide to count as one rule. Second, which robots are governed, and which are only studied? Third, what makes a robot rule different from a general AI rule? Fourth, do regions of the world differ? Fifth, what is changing over time? And a sixth question that arose from the study itself: does how you search change what you find? The answer to that one is yes, a lot.

## 5. How the documents were collected

The collection was built in two rounds. In round one, eight regional teams searched the web for anything about robot and AI governance. They found research papers, strategies and guidelines, but few actual laws: one thousand six hundred and fifty-six documents after duplicates were removed. In round two, nine AI search agents and seven scripts queried official law databases, standards catalogues and treaty archives for the laws the first round had missed, and added one thousand six hundred and forty-one documents. Together, that is three thousand two hundred and ninety-five documents from one hundred and sixteen countries, three quarters of them in English.

## 6. Round 1: eight teams searching the web

Let us look at round one more closely. Between 2025 and October 2026, teams for Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America, the Middle East, North America and Oceania, plus an international layer, ran two exercises: a sprint for robot-specific sources, and a database of general AI-governance instruments to serve as the comparison group. They recorded two thousand one hundred and sixty sources and captured one thousand eight hundred and ninety-four of them. After removing duplicates, round one held one thousand six hundred and fifty-six documents. What the teams found was research, strategies and guidelines. What they missed was the aviation, vehicle and machinery registers where binding law lives. Round one held sixty-five robot instruments, and only five of them were binding.

## 7. Round 2: AI agents and scripts searching legal databases

Round two looked where the law lives. Nine AI search agents, each with a regional or thematic brief and the round-one holdings as an exclusion list, were joined by scripted harvests of seven official databases: EU law, UK legislation, Japan's law portal, China's State Council library, Australia's Federal Register, the US Code of Federal Regulations and Brazil's Chamber of Deputies. No person curated the candidates. Two thousand and seventy-five candidates survived de-duplication; texts were obtained for one thousand seven hundred and twenty; and one thousand six hundred and twenty-six proved usable. Three hundred and fifty-five candidates never yielded a text, because of errors, blocked hosts or script-only pages, and they are counted nowhere.

## 8. How the documents were labelled

Every document was read by an AI model against a fixed questionnaire of thirty-nine fields, the same for all of them. What kind of document is this? Is it legally binding? Which machines does it cover? Which safety and ethics topics does it mention, and which does it spell out in detail? The model saw only the text, never the collector's notes, so it could not be steered by a label someone had already given. No person read or labelled any document. The collectors in round two, the reviewers of earlier drafts and the auditor were all AI agents too. The labels were checked for consistency: a second, different model agreed with them at a kappa of about point eight five, and the same model run again at point nine one, in both rounds. Treat every label as consistent, not certified.

## 9. The 39 questions asked of every document

Here are all thirty-nine questions, grouped. Ten identify who wrote it and when: title, language, issuer and its kind, year, month, where the date comes from, DOI and venue. Three say where it applies. Six say what kind of document it is: type, class, whether it is a governance document, its status, whether it binds, and its publication stage. Five cover AI and machines: scope, embodiment, the robot types, the main type, and sectors. Seven cover what it says: topics, the main topic, twenty principles each scored as absent, mentioned or spelled out, the governance tools, the other rules it cites, its position on legal personhood for robots, and how it frames risk. Three cover stance and method, and five cover the quality of the reading, including the model's own confidence.

## 10. The Chinese, Japanese and Korean texts had to be read again

One repair deserves its own slide. Before a document reaches the model, a cleaner strips menu lines from the top of web pages. The earlier rule dropped short lines with no spaces between words and no Western full stop. Chinese, Japanese and Korean paragraphs have neither. So for most Chinese documents, only a fraction of the text reached the model: a median of a quarter of each document. A reviewer caught it. Eighty-three Chinese documents that name robots or AI had been labelled "not about AI". The fix was a cleaner that never drops a line carrying those characters, and a re-reading of all four hundred and twenty-two such documents. Fifty-seven changed whether they count as about robots, and the number rated "not about AI" fell from two hundred and five to one hundred and sixty.

## 11. Four rules and 157 corrections

An expert reviewer read one hundred and fifty-nine entries of the earlier version, chosen where errors were likely, and found at least one error in sixty-eight percent of them. The response came in two parts. First, four rules applied to every document: a standard still in draft is not a published instrument; a mandatory Chinese national standard is binding law; a working paper from the UN weapons talks is a position document, not a draft rule; and a proposal whose adopted act is in the collection is superseded. Second, one hundred and fifty-seven named corrections on ninety-one documents, each recording the field, the new value, the reason and the source. One example: the 2006 Machinery Directive had been marked repealed. It is in force until January 2027, and was corrected.

## 12. Five words you need

Before the numbers, five words. An instrument is any official text meant to steer conduct, from a statute to a voluntary guideline. Binding means it has legal force: you can be fined or stopped for ignoring it. Soft law is guidance, strategies and standards, followed by choice or by contract. A principal instrument is a law or regulation counted once, with its amendments, but without its paperwork: no commencement orders, fee rules, exemptions, corrigenda, index pages or stubs under a hundred and fifty words. And the embodied set is the documents the model judged to be about AI in a body. Every number from here on says which of these it is counting.

## 13. What the two sets are made of

The model sorted the usable documents into a robot set of one thousand six hundred and three, a general-AI set of one thousand and fifteen, and the rest. The two sets are built from different kinds of documents. The robot set is one third research papers, one third principal instruments, and a fifth paperwork, index pages and stubs. The general-AI set is more than a third news and communications and a quarter soft-law strategies and guidelines, with only forty-eight research papers. Five hundred and forty-seven documents are in neither set, because the model judged them not to be about AI at all; most are harvested statutes with no AI content. This difference in composition is why every later comparison is also made within one kind of document at a time.

## 14. How many rules there are depends on how you count

Here is one collection read under successively stricter definitions. Count every binding legal text, paperwork included, and there are two hundred and ninety-one. Remove the paperwork and count principal instruments, and there are one hundred and ninety-five. Keep only those rated genuinely about robots: one hundred and sixty-eight. Keep only those whose text contains any provision on autonomy or AI: one hundred and forty. And keep only those that name artificial intelligence explicitly: fifteen. The ninety-six texts that leave between the first two rows are commencement orders, levies, exemptions, index pages and duplicates, which is simply how legal databases expose one regime as many entries.

## 15. Only 15 binding rules name AI

Fifteen. That is the number to remember. Of the one hundred and ninety-five binding rules that reach robots, fifteen name artificial intelligence at all. The other one hundred and eighty govern drones, vehicles and machines as machines. They were written for aviation, road traffic, factories and products, and they reach robots because robots are also aircraft, vehicles and products.

## 16. Where the binding rules come from

The United States supplies fifty-six of the one hundred and ninety-five binding rules, the European Union thirty-four, Japan twenty-two, the United Kingdom eleven and Canada nine; thirty-five other jurisdictions supply the remaining sixty-three. By kind, they are one hundred and thirty-two regulations or decrees, sixty statutes and three court decisions. One hundred and eighty-two are in force. And one hundred and ninety of the one hundred and ninety-five came from the second, database-driven round; the first round had found five. One caution: the collection over-represents places with open legal databases. France, Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and the Chinese provinces are thin because their law portals blocked the search.

## 17. Which jurisdictions hold the robot rules

Counting all principal robot instruments, binding and soft, the United States holds eighty-three, the European Union seventy-seven, the international standards bodies sixty-five, China fifty-five, Japan forty-six and the United Kingdom thirty-nine. Fifty-eight jurisdictions hold at least one, and forty hold a binding one. Two facts about shape: forty-two percent of all usable documents are European, and sixty-eight percent of the non-English documents are governance documents, against forty-nine percent of the English ones. The law came in other languages; the research came in English.

## 18. Which machines the binding rules cover

Of the one hundred and ninety-five binding rules, one hundred and seven mention drones and sixty-seven mention self-driving road vehicles. Then the numbers fall away: fifteen for delivery robots, thirteen for factory robot arms, eleven for security and police robots, four for care robots. Companion robots and humanoids have none. One rule can mention several machines, so the bars do not add up to the total.

## 19. Law reaches robots sideways

Almost no country has written a law about robots. Instead, robots are caught by laws written for the things they also are. Aviation law gives drone rules: registration, pilot licences, no-fly zones. Road-traffic law has been amended for automated driving, to say who the driver is when nobody drives. Machinery and workplace-safety law treats factory arms as machines with CE marks. Product and medical-device law treats surgical and consumer robots as products that must be safe. And general AI law, such as the EU AI Act, reaches robots through its product lists without naming them. The one robotics statute the study has read is El Salvador's Robotic Technologies Law of 2025. Hangzhou's robot regulations and Korea's Intelligent Robots Act were confirmed to exist, but sit on websites the search could not reach.

## 20. Which robots the documents talk about at all

Across all one thousand six hundred and three robot documents, papers, rules, reports and news together, self-driving vehicles are mentioned most, by four hundred and thirty-two, then drones by four hundred and fifteen. Companion and social robots come third with two hundred and forty-four, then care robots, professional service robots, factory arms, autonomous weapons, humanoids, surgical robots and collaborative robots. The orange bars, companion, care and humanoid robots, are the ones people will live with, and, as the next slides show, the ones the law has least to say about.

## 21. Research and rules point at different robots

Compare the five hundred and nine research papers with the five hundred and five principal instruments. Companion and social robots are the subject of forty-three percent of the papers and three percent of the instruments, a gap of forty points, with no binding rule at all. Humanoids and care robots show gaps of nineteen points each. Factory arms are near parity. Then the mirror image: self-driving vehicles are twelve percent of the research and forty percent of the instruments; drones six percent of the research and forty-two percent of the instruments. Scientists study the robots people will live with; lawmakers regulate the things that move through public space. Part of the drone and vehicle lead is the search itself, which looked for those terms in legal databases.

## 22. Different regions, different robots, and a shift over time

Which robots a region talks about depends on what its documents are. Companion and social robots appear in twenty-seven percent of Europe's robot documents and fifteen percent of North America's. Humanoids have their highest shares in the Middle East and Asia. Drones dominate Oceania, Africa and Latin America, whose documents are mostly aviation regulation. Over time, within the robot set, the share of documents mentioning companion robots fell from twenty percent in 2020 to 2022 to twelve percent in 2025 to 2026, while humanoids rose from eight to seventeen percent. Attention is moving from the robot as companion to the robot as general-purpose worker, and humanoids have no binding rule.

## 23. What makes a robot rule different

Now the heart of the comparison: five hundred and five robot instruments against three hundred and seventy-two general-AI instruments. Physical safety is raised by eighty-three percent of robot rules and nine percent of general-AI rules. Testing before sale: sixty-six against nineteen. How much the machine may decide alone: sixty against ten. How close it may work to people: thirty-three percent against a third of one percent. Who pays for damage: twenty-seven against three. Whether it could become a weapon: twenty-one against twelve. These six differences hold in both collection rounds, in research papers as well as rules, and after statistical adjustment. The body is what sets robot rules apart, and the study calls this its most reliable result.

## 24. Does the difference survive when like is compared with like?

A statistical model compares robot documents with general-AI documents of the same kind, region, year and collection round. The odds ratio says how many times more likely a concern is to appear in a robot document once those differences are held constant. Physical safety: forty-five times. Liability for damage: twenty-one. Autonomy and control: seventeen. Conformity assessment: six. Dual use: three. Eight core values, from fairness to sustainability, are less likely in robot documents after adjustment, and four show no difference. Without the collection round in the model, eleven looked lower; the three that the round removes are exactly the ones the within-round check showed to be artefacts of the second round. Read the remaining eight as a property of harvested legal texts, not of robot governance as such.

## 25. The tools robot rules use

Robot rules borrow the toolkit of product safety. Sixty-five percent refer to a technical standard, against thirty-five percent of general-AI rules. Forty-four percent use licensing and registration, against ten. Forty-two percent require testing before sale, against twelve. Fifteen percent require insurance, against two. Think of a car: a type approval, a registration plate, an insurance policy. General-AI rules reach instead for institutions and transparency: oversight bodies, audits and impact assessments, labelling and disclosure duties, voluntary codes, funding and education. Think of a data regulator: an agency, an audit, a notice that you are talking to a machine. Risk tiers, sandboxes and human-oversight duties are used about equally by both.

## 26. Mentioning a problem is not the same as solving it

For each concern, the light bar is the share of the one hundred and ninety-five binding rules that mention it, and the dark bar the share that spell out what must be done. Physical safety: mentioned by eighty-nine percent, spelled out by sixty-two. Testing before sale: seventy and forty-seven. How much the machine may decide: sixty-one and twenty-five. Who pays for damage: thirty-three and eighteen. How close to people: thirty and six. Binding law is good at safety and pre-market testing. The control question, how much a machine may decide and when a person must step in, is raised by six rules in ten and answered by one in four.

## 27. Technical standards were read from the cover, not the contents

Standards such as ISO 10218 for industrial robots are the operative safety texts, but their full text is sold, not published. The study could read only each standard's public scope statement, often a few hundred words. So every statement about standards describes what a scope page says, never what a clause requires, and standards are reported as their own measurement. On sixty-two scope pages, physical safety is mentioned by ninety-five percent and spelled out by sixty-eight; autonomy by sixty and twenty-three; liability almost never; dual use never. And of the eight hundred and ten robot legal texts, two hundred and twenty-two are catalogue pages, mostly standards listings, which enter no count at all.

## 28. The two worlds cite different rulebooks

General-AI documents cite a shared canon. Twenty-eight percent name the EU AI Act, twenty-one percent the GDPR, fourteen percent the OECD principles. Robot documents cite the AI Act in six percent of cases and the GDPR in eight, and reach instead for the UN weapons talks and the European Parliament's Civil Law Rules on Robotics, which general-AI documents almost never mention. Half of all general-AI documents anchor themselves to a named rule; three robot documents in four name none. The robot field has sector law, standards and research, but no shared reference text.

## 29. Ethics in Europe, risk in North America, liability in the Middle East

How a document frames the issue varies by region. Among robot documents that take a frame, Europe's are ethics-led, in thirty-six percent of cases. North America's and Oceania's are risk-led, Oceania strongly so at sixty-four percent. Asia's are split between promoting innovation and managing risk. The Middle East's are liability-led, at fifty-nine percent. And among the four hundred and eighty robot research papers that take a position on regulation, fifty-six percent take none, thirty-one percent call for new rules, and only four percent argue that existing law suffices. The research community rarely engages with the law that already applies.

## 30. Where you look changes what you find

This is the method lesson of the whole study. Version two, built from web search, reported five binding robot rules. Wrong: the laws live in legal registers, not on the open web. Version three, built from legal databases, counted two hundred and ninety-nine. Inflated: every fee rule and exemption counted as a law. Version four, counted properly, holds one hundred and ninety-five. Neither jump happened in the world; both happened in the method. The search also changed a conclusion. Version three reported that robot rules talk less about fairness, transparency and privacy than general-AI rules do. Looked at one round at a time, the difference appears only in the second round: among the first round's instruments, none of the fourteen values differs. Harvested legal texts are short and procedural; strategies found by web search are long on values. The so-called ethical deficit was a property of the documents, not of robot governance.

## 31. How regions differ

On tools, regions differ clearly: whether a document refers to standards, requires a licence or sets a liability regime varies most. On harm, they hardly differ at all: physical safety, avoiding harm and human oversight are mentioned at similar rates everywhere. On values, it depends what you compare. Among research papers, regions agree on fairness, transparency, privacy and beneficence; only two of fourteen core values differ. Among instruments, twelve of fourteen differ, with the standards-heavy international layer at the low end and Asia's strategies at the high end. So the difference is about what kind of document each region produces. And remember the shape of the collection: Europe is forty-two percent of everything. This is not a census of the world.

## 32. What is changing

Three movements. Rising: within the first collection, the odds that a document uses risk tiers grow by thirty-one percent a year, and pre-market testing by twenty-six percent; both are diluted, not reversed, by the harvested legal texts. Shifting: the robot-personhood debate is fading, with an odds ratio of point three nine for 2025 to 2026 against the earlier period, while industrial policy rises. Arriving: thirteen percent of 2026 robot documents use the terms embodied AI or physical AI, up from one percent in 2024. And one trend that turned out not to be one: pooled over both rounds, attention to proximity and liability looked like it was declining six percent a year. Within the first collection it is flat. The decline was the arrival of short legal texts that never mention either.

## 33. Seven ways the labels were checked against themselves

Kappa measures agreement beyond chance: zero is chance, one is perfect, above point eight is usually called strong. In round one, a second model agreed with the labels at point eight five, the same model run again at point nine one, the same model reading the full text at point eight eight, a run with all metadata hidden at point nine zero, and the longer re-annotation of governance texts at point eight three. In round two, a second model agreed at point eight six and a re-run at point nine one. Agreement is weakest on judging page quality, a document's stance toward regulation, its publication stage, and whether a rule is still in force. Two more checks: the round-two collectors' own catalogue data agree with the blind labels on binding nature in ninety-three percent of cases, and simple word lists over the full text rank the principles the same way as the labels do. But all of these are machine against machine. They show the labels are stable, not that an expert would agree.

## 34. The audit: 100 binding rules, re-read one by one

An independent AI reader took a random sample of one hundred of the two hundred and ninety-nine texts the earlier version had counted as binding robot rules, and judged five questions from each text. Is it a distinct instrument, not paperwork? Sixty-seven percent. Is it legally binding? Ninety-seven. Does it say something substantive about a machine with a body? Fifty-six. Any provision on autonomy or AI? Sixty-seven. Does it name AI explicitly? Fourteen. All of the first three at once: fifty-four percent, with a plausible range of forty-four to sixty-three. So of the two hundred and ninety-nine, about one hundred and sixty-one were genuine. The counting rules that replaced that count keep one hundred and ninety-five, and the two bracket the truth. Rules and auditor agree on seventy-seven of the hundred; rules cannot see whether a text has an operative robot clause.

## 35. The coverage audit: 39 rules everyone knows, how many are in

Recall is tested against a list of thirty-nine well-known robot, drone and vehicle instruments. Twenty-two are present as a principal instrument whose text was read; thirty-one on a looser test; the first round alone had eleven. Still missing on the strict test are Germany's autonomous-driving law, Korea's Intelligent Robots Act and AI Basic Act, the revised EU Product Liability Directive, Shenzhen's connected-vehicle regulation, Beijing's humanoid-robot measures, and eight safety standards. A further group was confirmed to exist but could not be downloaded: Hangzhou's robot regulations, Hong Kong's autonomous-vehicle regulation, Korea's decrees, India's Drone Rules, France's vehicle-liability ordinance and others. Their hosts blocked the search; they head the list for a third round.

## 36. How far to trust these numbers

Four cautions. Even one hundred and ninety-five is a ceiling: the audit found fifty-four percent of the earlier binding set to be genuine, so about one binding rule in four may still fail a careful reading. It is also a floor: twenty-two of thirty-nine well-known instruments are in, and one hundred and eighty-nine more were confirmed but could not be downloaded. No person labelled anything: every label, collector, reviewer and auditor was an AI model from one company, so the checks show consistency, not expert agreement. And the collection is lopsided: three quarters English, forty-two percent European, and the countries with open legal databases supply one hundred and twelve of the one hundred and ninety-five binding rules. The study was reviewed by six adversarial reviewers before this version, and every number was audited against the data. The reviews and the audit ship with the release.

## 37. What it means for three people

A worker beside a robot arm is covered, but by machinery and workplace law, not robot law: ISO 10218 sets the safety requirements, and in Europe the Machinery Regulation requires testing and a CE mark before sale. Someone buying a companion robot for a parent finds no binding rule written for it; what applies is the EU AI Act's bans on manipulative systems and its duty to tell people they are talking to a machine, product-safety and data-protection law, and a voluntary care-robot standard. A city buying delivery robots meets real rules: US state statutes that admit them to sidewalks under weight, speed and insurance conditions, and Japan's Road Traffic Act, which treats small remote-operated vehicles as pedestrians. The pattern repeats: the more a robot resembles something already regulated, the more law it meets.

## 38. What a third round would do

The paper lists what it could not do, each a known gap with a known fix. First, read what is blocked: fetch the verified-but-unread instruments from Korea, China's provinces, Hong Kong, France and India by other routes. Second, have people label a sample, turning the consistency checks into a validity check. Third, date the versions, so that old statutes stop carrying old dates for new robot clauses. Fourth, read the longest laws in full, beyond the hundred-and-ten-thousand-character cut. Fifth, record whom a rule binds, when it applies, and the legal effect of a standard. Sixth, split the research-versus-rules gap by collection source, to show how much of it is real.

## 39. What was released, and what it cost

Everything needed to check a claim ships with the study: the master table with every field for every document; every label with its model, prompt and cost, and the exact text the model read; the codebook, the counting rules, the one hundred and fifty-seven corrections with reasons; all tables, figures, scripts and the dashboard; and the six adversarial reviews, the precision audit and the change log. Not released: the source texts themselves, for copyright reasons. The whole labelling effort, three thousand two hundred and ninety-five documents, a re-reading of four hundred and twenty-two, and six consistency checks, cost three hundred and thirty-four US dollars. One caveat: the model's randomness cannot be fixed, so re-runs reproduce the analysis from the stored labels, not the labels themselves.

## 40. Explore the data yourself

Everything in this talk can be explored directly. The dashboard holds every document, searchable and filterable by region, machine type, kind of rule and collection round; click a row to read its summary. The full paper, the analysis report, the data tables, the reviews and the scripts that reproduce every figure are in the public repository on GitHub. Both links are on the slide.

## 41. Five things to remember

Five things to take away. One: robots are governed sideways. Of one hundred and ninety-five binding rules that reach them, fifteen name AI; the rest are aviation, road, machinery, product and medical-device law. Two: the body is what makes a robot rule different: physical safety, testing before sale, how much the machine may decide, and who pays for damage. Three: research and law look at different robots; companion robots are forty-three percent of papers and three percent of rules. Four: mention is not detail; six binding rules in ten raise the control question, one in four answers it. Five: where you look changes what you find. Five, then two hundred and ninety-nine, then one hundred and ninety-five binding rules from the same world. Any count of AI regulation should say how it was collected and what it counts. Thank you.
