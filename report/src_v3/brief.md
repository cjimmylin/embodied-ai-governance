---
title: "Who governs the robots? What 3,291 documents from eight world regions say"
subtitle: "A short brief (v3: after independent review and a second collection round)"
author: "Centre for Enterprise Innovation (CEI) AI Robotics project, with Claude Code"
date: "31 October 2026"
---

**Almost every binding rule that reaches a robot was written for something that flies or drives.** Of the 299 legally binding instruments in this collection that cover robots or other "embodied AI" (AI in a body), 265 (89%) govern drones or automated road vehicles. The other 34 reach robots through machinery, workplace-safety, medical-device, export-control and delivery-device law; only one, El Salvador's Robotic Technologies Law (in force since July 2025), is a national law written for robots as such. That is the central finding of a study of 3,291 documents (laws, standards, guidelines, reports, research and news) collected by eight regional teams and a second, database-driven round, and labelled by an AI model. No human read every document; a second AI model agreed with the first on about nine labels in ten, and the labels should be read as consistent, not certified.

**The big numbers.** 1,589 of the 3,148 usable documents are about robots or other embodied AI. Of those, 808 are rules or policies of any kind, 681 written for robots specifically (the other 127 are general-AI instruments with robot provisions), and 299 legally binding (268 in force, 24 superseded, 7 adopted but not yet applying). These are counts *within this collection*: an audit against 38 known instruments finds 31 (12 in v2); still missing are Korea's 2008 Intelligent Robots Act, the EU's revised Product Liability Directive, the two American industrial-robot standards, Korea's AI Basic Act, the OECD AI Principles and Taiwan's industrial-robot hazard standards, so every count is a lower bound.

**What round 2 changed.** The first collection held 65 robot instruments, 5 of them binding. Nine collector agents and scripted harvests of seven legal databases, plus standards catalogues and regulator sites, found 2,075 candidates; 1,716 had text and were labelled blind as before, 1,611 are usable, 1,346 are governance instruments, 647 robot-specific. The robot set grew from 675 to 1,589 documents, the general-AI set only from 806 to 982, and research papers barely moved (508 to 509): round 2 added rules, not studies. Of its 294 new binding robot instruments, 162 are drone rules and 92 automated-driving rules. Details are in the companion report.

**Scientists study companion robots. Lawmakers regulate things that move.** Companion and social robots are the subject of 43% of the robot-governance research papers (219 of 509) but of 13 of the 812 robot governance documents (2%), none binding. Drones are 6% of research (32) and 38% of governance documents (312; 172 binding); self-driving cars 12% (63) and 36% (293; 100 binding). Care robots, balanced in v2, now show a gap: 28% of research (140) against 6% of governance documents (49; 4 binding). Industrial robot arms are the one large category where the two match (10% and 13%; 51 and 105). Uncertainty intervals exclude zero for every gap but the industrial one. Humanoids have 164 documents, 27 instruments, one text labelled binding (a 2025 Beijing development-zone measure) and one bill (Brazil, 2026).

**Robot rules are machinery rules; AI rules are audit rules.** Among governance documents (812 robot, 373 general-AI), those about robots refer to technical standards (64% vs 36%), require pre-market conformity assessment (31% vs 11%) and licences or registration (36% vs 6%). General-AI rules rely more on audits, labelling and data governance; testing and sandboxes, a v2 difference, no longer differ (27% vs 30%). Robot documents of every kind (1,589 vs 982) raise physical safety (67% vs 4%), human-robot proximity (43% vs 0.3%), liability for physical damage (21% vs 1%) and how much autonomy a machine may keep (49% vs 5%), differences that survive adjustment for genre, region and year (odds 4 to 170 times). The ethical core no longer evens out as in v2: allowing for genre, robot documents discuss fairness (26% vs 59% overall), transparency (36% vs 59%) and privacy (27% vs 50%) less than general-AI documents; only robustness and security match.

**Everyone raises the control question; few write it down.** "How much autonomy may a robot keep, and when must a human be in the loop?" is raised by 43% of technical standards (n=238), 52% of binding rules (299), 59% of soft-law instruments (196) and 69% of drafts (75). It is set out in detail by about one in five binding rules (18%) and guidelines (21%), one in eight standards (13%) and a third of drafts (37%). Physical safety is what binding rules and standards both specify in depth (46% and 47%); standards remain the texts the policy literature cites least (ISO 10218 is cited by 42 of 1,589 robot documents).

**Regions differ on tools, and now on values too.** Across six regions (n=1,527 robot documents), the items that vary least are audits, self-regulation codes, human-oversight requirements and labelling (no significant regional difference) and, among values, dual use, human oversight and autonomy-level control; benefit, trust, privacy, references to standards (18% to 86% of governance documents by region), licensing (5% to 72%) and liability regimes vary most. Liability for physical damage runs from 4% of robot documents in the international standards layer (n=241) to 58% in the Middle East (n=45). Europe, North America and Asia form the closest group (n=645, 217, 296; Europe and North America, indistinguishable in v2, are now merely close); Oceania, here mostly Australian drone regulation (n=83), and the standards layer stand furthest apart. Sixty jurisdictions hold a robot instrument (30 in v2); among national jurisdictions the United States (100) and China (94) hold the most, though the international standards layer (198) and the EU (106) hold more.

**What is changing.** Between robot documents of 2020 to 2024 (n=737) and 2025 to 2026 (n=476), the share on industrial policy rose from 22% to 31% and, unlike in v2, the rise survives allowing for the changing mix of documents (odds 1.6 times, at the edge of significance after correction); the robot-personhood debate kept falling (10% to 5%; odds 0.39). Two v2 trends have gone: cybersecurity (17% to 19%) and human oversight (24% to 26%) no longer move. Risk-based tiering appears about 9% more often each year, at the edge of significance; conformity assessment's v2 growth is no longer significant. Humanoids rose from 8% of robot documents in 2023 to 2024 (n=341) to 16% in 2025 to 2026 (n=476), and the terms "embodied AI" or "physical AI" from 1% of robot documents in 2024 (2 of 191) to 12% in 2026 (31 of 260).

**What it means for you.** A factory worker beside a collaborative robot: ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 and, depending on where you work, machinery law (17 binding instruments reach industrial arms). Someone buying a companion robot for a parent: 13 instruments, none binding; what reaches the purchase is general AI law, data-protection law and the care-robot standard ISO 13482. A city buying delivery robots: 15 binding rules, mostly US state "personal delivery device" statutes and Japan's road-traffic rules. A hospital buying a surgical robot: medical-device law and the AI Act's medical-device provisions.

**Read with care.** 42% of the documents are European (1,332 of 3,148) and 75% English; 266 are in Chinese, nearly all from round 2 (7 before it). The collection sought robot research, general-AI official texts and, in round 2, instruments, so the ratio of papers to rules is by design. Every label was assigned by an AI model (Claude Sonnet 5.5) from excerpts and checked against a second model, a re-run, a full-text re-read and a version with the collectors' labels removed; it was not checked by human coders. Round 2's limits: 46 candidates sat on blocked hosts and 273 returned errors or script-only pages, and are counted nowhere; the collectors' shared search budget ran out part-way through every brief; and technical standards are annotated on their public scope text only, never the normative text. 2026 runs to October. All counts of instruments are lower bounds. Version 3 adds the second collection round to version 2, which followed six adversarial reviews; the companion report lists every change.
