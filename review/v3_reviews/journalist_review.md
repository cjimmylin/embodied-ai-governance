## 1. The story I would write (three sentences)

An AI-assisted survey of 3,291 laws, standards and papers from eight world regions finds that the rules which bind robots were almost all written for something else: of 299 legally binding instruments that reach a robot, nine in ten govern drones or self-driving cars, and only one country, El Salvador, has a national law for robots as such. The robots people actually live with fare worst: companion, care and humanoid robots are the subject of 413 documents and more than 40% of the research, yet the study's own list of "binding rules" that touch them is five items long and includes the EU AI Act, a US medical-device classification and a 34-word Beijing subsidy notice. The authors, who had an AI model read and label every document and AI search agents gather most of the laws, call their count a floor; a sceptical reader should add that it is also a count of regulatory paperwork (Australian drone exemption notices alone are one in eight of the 299) rather than of regulatory regimes, and that it says as much about where the team searched as about how the world governs robots.

**Headlines I could file**

1. "Scientists study companion robots. Lawmakers regulate drones." (219 of 509 papers vs 13 of 808 instruments, none binding; drones 32 papers vs 312 instruments, 172 binding. Figure 18, Table 22.)
2. "One country has a robot law. Everyone else borrows from aviation, road and factory rules." (El Salvador's Robotic Technologies Law, Decree 340, 2025; the other 298 binding instruments reach robots through sector law. Supplement Table S01.)
3. "Everyone asks how much control a robot may keep. One law in five answers." (Autonomy-level control raised by 52% of binding rules, specified in depth by 18%. Table 9.) Hold the standards half of this line until finding C4 is fixed.

What I would not file: anything with "299" or "89%" in it until C1 is addressed, and anything about ISO standards "specifying" safety (C4).
