Five ways to show one instrument

Each proposal renders the same database entry, built from the 39 codebook fields plus the derived fields version 4 added, for one real instrument: the EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230. The reader is someone learning how physical AI is governed, not a lawyer. Pick the proposal, or the mix, that fits the reference tool you want.

What differsNot the data but the reading order: fact sheet, passport, scorecard, questions, or lifecycle. Each ends with who it serves and what it costs.

Proposal 1

The dossier: a one-page fact sheet

Everything on one scrolling page in a fixed order: identity band, what it is, which machines, what it requires, how the 20 principles score, where the record came from. The order follows the seven groups of the codebook, so every entry reads the same way.

Who it serves

Policy analysts and students who want the whole picture of one instrument without clicking.

  • Strength: nothing hidden; prints well; the principle grid is scannable.
  • Trade-off: long; a newcomer meets raw field names early; no comparison to other entries.

    Proposal 2

    The passport: identity card plus four tabs

    A compact identity card never changes: name, issuer, status, scope, a machine-readable strip. The 39 fields sit behind four tabs (What it is, Where it applies, What it says, How we know), so the reader opens only the layer they need.

    Who it serves

    People scanning many instruments quickly: the card is the same shape for all 505, so the eye learns where to look.

    • Strength: compact; consistent; the strip holds ids and hashes for citation.
    • Trade-off: tabs hide content; not printable in one go; needs a click to reach the principles.

    Proposal 3

    The scorecard: this instrument against its peers

    The same fields, but every scored or multi-choice field is drawn against the 195 principal binding robot rules in the collection. The reader sees at once what is typical (physical safety) and what is unusual (risk tiers, transparency duties) about this instrument.

    Who it serves

    Researchers and journalists asking “is this rule unusual?”; teachers showing what a strong instrument looks like.

    • Strength: context on every line; the marker-on-bar idiom needs no legend after the first row.
    • Trade-off: the peer group must be chosen and stated; identity fields become secondary; percentages invite over-reading of model labels.

      Proposal 4

      The questions: a plain-language walk-through

      The entry is a list of the questions a newcomer actually asks, each answered in a sentence, with the raw field values shown beneath as chips. The 39 fields are all present, but grouped by question rather than by schema.

      Who it serves

      First-time readers: students, journalists, product managers who have never read a regulation.

      • Strength: no jargon before its explanation; the raw chips teach the schema gradually.
      • Trade-off: the sentences are written per entry (or generated and checked); long for experts; hard to scan 500 of them.

      Proposal 5

      The lifecycle: where this instrument sits in time and among others

      The entry opens on a timeline (proposal, adoption, entry into force, application, repeal of the predecessor) and a map of relations: what it replaces, what it cites, what cites it, and the family of implementing decisions around it. The 39 fields follow as a compact ledger.

      Who it serves

      Anyone tracking change: compliance teams, legislative analysts, people who ask “is this still the law?”

      • Strength: status and dates become visible facts, not labels; relations turn 505 entries into a network.
      • Trade-off: dates beyond adoption are not in the codebook today (application date, repeal date) and must be added; the relation graph depends on the citation field, which the model fills unevenly.
