Who governs the robots? The keynote

What 3,320 documents from 119 countries say about the rules for machines that act in the physical world, told for people who are new to robots, to AI and to law. Twenty slides, ten headlines, five things to take home.

Script

1Cover

Thank you. I want to spend the next twenty minutes on a question that sounds simple and turns out not to be: who governs the robots? Not the chatbots. Not the recommendation engines. The machines. The drone over the stadium, the car with nobody at the wheel, the arm on the factory floor, and, soon, the robot in your parent's care home. We went looking for every rule in the world that reaches those machines, and for every paper and strategy written about them. We found three thousand three hundred and twenty documents from a hundred and nineteen countries. I am going to tell you what they say, and I am going to be honest about how much we can trust what they say. You do not need any background in robotics, in AI or in law. I will give you ten headlines, and then I will tell you what I think they mean.

2The question

Here is why the body matters. When a piece of software goes wrong, you get a bad sentence, a bad score, a bad recommendation. Serious things, but reversible. When an AI with a body goes wrong, you get an event. A collision. A crushed hand. A fall in a hallway at two in the morning. The questions change the moment the intelligence can move. Can it hurt someone? A two-hundred-kilogram robot arm can. A one-and-a-half-tonne car can. How close may it work to a person? Who pays when it breaks something, or someone? And the hardest one: how much may the machine decide on its own before a human has to step in? Those four questions are not the questions in most AI ethics guidelines, which are about bias, privacy and transparency. So we asked something plain. Anywhere in the world, what rules exist for AI with a body, and what do they actually say?

3What we did

We built a map. Three thousand three hundred and twenty documents: laws, regulations, standards, strategies, guidelines, research papers and news. A hundred and nineteen countries, the European Union and the international bodies. The collection took three rounds. Regional teams searched the web first. Then AI agents searched the official legal databases, where the binding law actually lives, and found most of it. Then a third round went back for the texts the databases had blocked. Every document was read by an AI model against the same thirty-nine questions. What kind of document is this? Is it binding? Which machines does it cover? What does it say about safety, about control, about who is liable? Two things to hold in mind for the rest of the talk. First, this is a map, not a census. Every count I give you is a floor. Second, no person read every document. The labels came from AI, were checked by a second AI, and were audited by an independent AI reader. Treat them as consistent, not certified. I will come back to that.

4Headline one: robots are governed sideways

Headline one. Robots are governed sideways. We found two hundred and seventy-eight binding rules in the world that reach robots: statutes, regulations and decrees with legal force, each counted once, with their amendments but without their paperwork. Of those two hundred and seventy-eight, twenty-five name artificial intelligence at all. Two hundred and one contain some provision about autonomy or automation. The rest are aviation law, road-traffic law, machinery law, product-safety law and medical-device law. They reach robots not because anyone sat down to write a robot law, but because a drone is legally an aircraft, a self-driving car is legally a vehicle, and a robot arm is legally a machine. Almost none of them were written with AI in mind. If you came to this talk expecting a wave of robot regulation, this is the first surprise. The wave exists, but it is mostly old law catching new machines.

5Headline one, continued: what the rules are about

Look at what those two hundred and seventy-eight rules are about. A hundred and forty-six address drones. A hundred and nine address self-driving road vehicles. Twenty-nine address sidewalk delivery robots, mostly American state statutes that define a personal delivery device and tell it how fast it may go. Then the numbers fall off a cliff. Fourteen for security and policing robots. Thirteen for factory arms. Twelve for warehouse robots. Nine for farm machines. Four for care robots. Zero for companion robots. Zero for humanoids. The law reaches the machines that move through public space, where a collision is visible and a regulator already exists. It has not reached the machines that are about to move into private space.

6Headline two: the robots we will live with have almost no rules

That is headline two, and it is the one I most want you to remember. The robots we will live with have almost no rules. Zero binding rules for companion and social robots. Zero for humanoids. Four for care and assistive robots. Across the whole collection, four hundred and twenty-nine documents discuss those three kinds of machine. Four of them are binding law, and those four are general laws that mention care robots in passing: a Salvadoran robotics act, a Korean robot-industry act, and two Japanese special-zone ordinances. The one text written specifically for care robots is a voluntary standard, ISO 13482, from 2014. That is the whole legal inheritance of the robot that will help your mother out of bed.

7Headline two, continued: research and rules point at different robots

Now put the research next to the rules. This chart shows what share of five hundred and nine research papers, in blue, and what share of five hundred and ninety-eight governance instruments, in copper, address each kind of robot. Companion and social robots: forty-three percent of the research, three percent of the rules. Care robots: twenty-eight and nine. Humanoids: twenty-three and four. Now the bottom of the chart. Self-driving vehicles: twelve percent of the research, forty-one percent of the rules. Drones: six and forty-three. Scientists study the robots people will live with. Lawmakers regulate the things that already move through streets and skies. We checked whether this gap was an artefact of where we searched, because the legal databases were queried for drones and vehicles. It is not. For companions, carers and humanoids, the gap holds whichever way the instruments were collected.

8Headline three: the body changes the rulebook

Headline three. The body changes the rulebook. We coded every one of the twenty values and concerns in these documents as absent, mentioned, or spelled out in detail. Here is what the binding rules spell out. Physical safety: a hundred and sixty-seven of two hundred and seventy-eight in detail. Testing and certification before sale: a hundred and thirty-two. Who is accountable: a hundred and thirty. Human oversight: eighty-three. The level of autonomy allowed: seventy-three. Liability for damage: sixty-three. Now the right-hand column, the vocabulary of AI ethics. Privacy in detail: forty-four. Transparency: eight. Fairness: three. Trust: three. Human dignity: one. Sustainability: none. This is not a criticism of the law. It is a description of it. When the intelligence has a body, the rulebook is about the body: can it hurt you, was it tested, who answers for it. The values that dominate the AI ethics literature barely appear.

9Headline four: mention is not detail

Headline four. Mention is not detail. The control question, how much the machine may decide before a person must step in, is the question that makes a robot rule a robot rule. A hundred and seventy-one of the binding rules raise it. Seventy-three answer it, in the sense of saying what is allowed, by whom, and checked how. Human oversight: a hundred and seventy-four raise it, eighty-three answer it. So six rules in ten raise the question and fewer than three in ten answer it. And most of the answers are in aviation and road-vehicle law, where words like remote pilot and fallback driver already have legal meaning. Everywhere else, the law gestures. It says there shall be appropriate human control, and it stops. That gap between mention and detail is where the next decade of litigation will live.

10Headline five: whom the rules bind

Headline five is new in this version of the study. We went back to every rule and asked it a question the codebook had not asked: by your own words, who has to obey you? Sixty-three percent name public bodies. Sixty-two percent name operators and deployers, the companies that run the machine. Thirty-nine percent name manufacturers. Thirty-eight, owners and users. Twenty-five, pilots and drivers. Eighteen percent name individuals, the general public, you. And eight percent, one rule in twelve, name the developers and providers of the software that does the deciding. The duty sits with whoever runs the machine in the world. The people who write the model that steers it are almost nowhere in the law.

11Headline six: the rules are new, and they arrive after the machines

Headline six. These rules are new, and they arrive after the machines. Three quarters of the binding rules date from 2019 or later. The three years from 2024 to October 2026 alone account for a hundred and five of the two hundred and seventy-eight. Look at the pattern. Drone rules followed drones into the sky, mostly between 2016 and 2021, after the near-misses at airports. Self-driving rules followed the test fleets onto the road. Delivery-robot statutes followed sidewalk robots into American cities between 2018 and 2022. Humanoids and companion robots have now arrived, in showrooms, in pilot programmes, in national strategies. On this pattern, their rules will follow too, and on this pattern, they will follow the incidents. That is the part we can still change.

12Headline seven: three regions write almost all of it

Headline seven. Three regions write almost all of it, and they write different things. North America has eighty-five of the binding rules, Europe eighty-two, Asia sixty-nine. Latin America sixteen, Oceania seven, Africa seven, the Middle East six. The United States legislates state by state: delivery robots in Pennsylvania, autonomous vehicles in Michigan, drones over prisons in a dozen places. The European Union regulates machines as products: the Machinery Regulation, the drone regulations, and the AI Act reaching robots as safety components. And Japan, China and Korea, with fifty-seven rules between them, are the only places on earth with laws that say robot in the title. Korea's Intelligent Robots Act. Japan's Level-4 amendments to its road-traffic law. Chinese city regulations for driverless cars, and the 2025 and 2026 plans for what Beijing and Shanghai call embodied intelligence. If you want to see what robot-specific law looks like, look east.

13Headline eight: the safety knowledge is for sale

Headline eight. The detailed safety knowledge is in standards, and standards are for sale. We found sixty-one technical standards in the collection, documents that say how to make a robot safe: ISO 10218 for factory arms, ISO 13482 for personal-care robots, ISO 3691-4 for driverless trucks, UL 4600 for autonomous products. Forty-five declare themselves voluntary. One is referenced by a law, which makes it binding in effect. A law says be safe. The standard says how: how fast, how close, how much force, what to test. And fifty-seven of the sixty-one could only be read from their public preview page, because the full text costs money. Nobody can read the standard without paying for it, and neither could we. The most practical safety knowledge about robots is the least public.

14Headline nine: where you look changes what you find

Headline nine, and this one is about us, and about every number you have ever seen on AI regulation. Where you look changes what you find. After round one, regional teams searching the web, we had five binding robot rules. After AI agents searched the legal databases, counting every legal text, two hundred and ninety-nine. After we removed the paperwork, the commencement orders, the fee schedules, the index pages that databases serve up as separate entries, a hundred and ninety-five. After we recovered a hundred and eight texts the databases had blocked, two hundred and seventy-eight. Same world. Four counts. Every count of AI regulation you have seen depends on where someone looked and what they decided was one rule. Ours does too. The difference is that we report it, and you can rerun it.

15Headline ten: AI read the law about AI

Headline ten. AI read the law about AI. How far can you trust it? Three numbers. Two different AI readers agreed with each other on a sample of a hundred and sixty documents at a kappa of point eight six; the same reader run twice agreed at point nine two. That is consistency. Then we took a random hundred of the binding rules and gave them to an independent AI auditor with three strict tests: is it a distinct instrument, is it binding, does it contain a substantive provision about a machine? Seventy-three passed, up from fifty-four a version earlier. What failed is instructive. Laws that mention a drone in a list of an agency's duties. Register pages captured instead of the statute. Two copies of the same EU regulation. The machine reads consistently; it does not yet read like a lawyer. And the third number is zero: the number of documents a human has labelled so far. The validation kit for that is ready. Every figure in this talk is a well-measured estimate with an honest error bar. None of it is a fact.

16What it means for AI research

So what does it mean? Three things for the people in this room who do research. First, the research agenda and the legal agenda are inverted. Four papers in ten about robot governance are about companion robots. Three rules in a hundred are. All our evidence on human-robot interaction, on deception, on attachment, on dependency in care, has nowhere in law to land. Second, autonomy is a legal word now, and it is underspecified. A hundred and seventy-one rules invoke it; seventy-three define it. The researchers who can translate levels of autonomy into testable conditions are writing the next decade of law, whether they intend to or not. Third, reading law at scale is now possible, and it needs the same rigour as any instrument: blind reading, agreement between readers, independent audits, counting units stated in advance. Governance can be measured, not only argued. That is a methods agenda, and it is open.

17What it means for governance

And three moves for the people who make rules. One. Write the rules for the robots we will live with before the incidents, not after. Companions, carers and humanoids have zero, zero and four. Drones got their rules after the near-misses. The pattern is avoidable this time, because we can see it coming. Two. Answer the control question; do not just raise it. Who may let the machine decide what, under which conditions, checked by whom. Aviation law shows it can be done. The other sectors mostly gesture. Three. Make the safety knowledge public. When a law points to a standard, the standard becomes law in effect. Fifty-seven of our sixty-one standards are behind a paywall. Rules that people cannot read are rules that people cannot follow.

18The honest edges of the map

Before the takeaways, the edges of the map. Seventy-four official sources refused our crawler, mostly in France, Norway, Korea, Taiwan and the Chinese provinces. Our crawler says who it is and does not pretend to be a browser, so those texts are listed for a person with ordinary access. Old statutes hide their robot provisions: a road-traffic act from 1960 can carry a 2023 article on automated driving, and we could date the provision in only twenty-eight of the two hundred and seventy-eight rules, so our trend lines are floors, not histories. English is seventy-four percent of the collection; we read Chinese, Japanese, Spanish and Portuguese in the original, but the search was richest in English and in the EU. And no human has labelled a document. The next step in this study is a person, not a model.

19Five things to take home

Five things to take home. One. Robots are governed sideways: two hundred and seventy-eight binding rules reach them, twenty-five name AI, and the rest are aviation, road, machinery and product law. Two. The robots we will live with have almost none: companions zero, humanoids zero, care robots four, among four hundred and twenty-nine documents about them. Three. The body changes the rulebook: physical safety, certification, accountability and liability lead; transparency, fairness and dignity barely appear. Four. Mention is not detail: six rules in ten raise the control question, fewer than three in ten answer it, and the duties fall on operators, almost never on the people who write the software. Five. Every count depends on where you looked. Ours went five, two hundred and ninety-nine, a hundred and ninety-five, two hundred and seventy-eight. Ask any number about AI regulation how it was made.

20Close

I will end where I began. The question is not whether robots will come into our homes. The strategies are written, the factories are tooling up, the demonstrations are on every feed. The question is whether the rules will be there when they do. Right now, for the machines that will share our kitchens and our care homes, the answer is no, and the gap is measured. The data, the code, every table and the list of what still needs a human are public, at the address on the screen, and you can explore all three thousand three hundred and twenty documents yourself. I would rather you check our numbers than believe them. Thank you.
