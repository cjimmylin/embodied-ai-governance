Keynote · AI research and AI governance

Who governs
the robots?

What 3,320 documents from 119 countries say about the rules for machines that act in the physical world, and the gap those rules leave open

Centre for Enterprise Innovation (CEI), AI Robotics project
Study version 5, November 2026

A talk for people who are new to robots, to AI and to law

The question

Software AI makes decisions. Embodied AI makes decisions and then moves.

A chatbot that goes wrong produces a bad sentence. A robot, a drone or a self-driving car that goes wrong produces a bad event: a collision, a crushed hand, a fall. The questions change the moment the AI has a body.

Can it hurt someone?

How close may it work to people?

Who pays when it breaks something?

How much may it decide alone?

So we asked a plain question: anywhere in the world, what rules exist for AI with a body, and what do they actually say?

What we did

We built a map of the world's robot rules, and let AI read it

3,320

documents: laws, standards, strategies, research papers and news

119

countries, the EU and the international bodies

3

collection rounds: regional teams, then AI agents searching legal databases, then recovering what was blocked

39

questions put to every document by an AI reader, the same for all

Two things to hold in mind. The collection is a map, not a census: it is a lower bound on what exists. And no person read every document; the labels were produced by AI, checked by a second AI, and audited by an independent AI reader. Treat them as consistent, not certified.

Headline four

Mention is not detail

The control question, how much the machine may decide before a person must step in, is the question that makes a robot rule a robot rule. Here is how often the binding rules raise it, and how often they answer it.

171

rules raise the level of autonomy the machine may have

73

rules spell it out: what is allowed, by whom, checked how

174

rules raise human oversight

83

rules spell it out

Six rules in ten raise the question; fewer than three in ten answer it. Most of the answers are in drone and vehicle law, where 'remote pilot' and 'fallback driver' already have legal meaning.

The United States (75 rules) legislates state by state: delivery robots, autonomous vehicles, drones over prisons.

The European Union (40) regulates machines as products: the Machinery Regulation, drone regulations, the AI Act reaching safety components.

Japan, China and Korea (57 together) are the only places with laws that say 'robot' in the title: Korea's Intelligent Robots Act, Japan's Level-4 traffic rules, China's city regulations for driverless cars, and the 2025 to 2026 plans for 'embodied intelligence' in Beijing and Shanghai.

Headline eight

The detailed safety knowledge is in standards, and standards are for sale

61

technical standards in the collection that say how to make a robot safe

45

declare themselves voluntary; 1 is referenced by law

57

could only be read from their public preview page

ISO 10218 for factory arms, ISO 13482 for personal-care robots, ISO 3691-4 for driverless trucks, UL 4600 for autonomous products: this is where the engineering detail lives. A law says 'be safe'; the standard says how. Nobody can read the standard without paying for it, and neither could we. The most practical safety knowledge about robots is the least public.

0.86

73%

of a random 100 of the binding rules survived an independent re-read on three strict tests, up from 54% a version earlier

What failed the audit is instructive: laws that mention a drone in a list of agency duties, register pages captured instead of the statute, two copies of the same EU regulation. The machine reads consistently; it does not yet read like a lawyer. Treat every number here as a well-measured estimate with an honest error bar, not a fact.

What it means

For AI research: we are studying robots that nobody is regulating

1

The research agenda and the legal agenda are inverted. Four papers in ten on robot governance are about companion robots; three rules in a hundred are. Our evidence on human-robot interaction, deception, attachment and dependency has nowhere in law to land.

2

'Autonomy' is a legal word now, and it is underspecified. 171 rules invoke it, 73 define it. Researchers who can translate levels of autonomy into testable conditions are writing the next decade of law whether they mean to or not.

3

Reading law at scale is now possible, and it needs the same rigour as any instrument. Blind reading, inter-reader agreement, independent audits, counting units stated in advance. Governance science can be measured, not only argued.

What it means

For governance: three moves before the next machines arrive

Write the rules for the robots we will live with, before the incidents

Companions, carers and humanoids have zero, zero and four binding rules. Drones got theirs after near-misses at airports. The pattern is avoidable.

Answer the control question, do not just raise it

Who may let the machine decide what, under which conditions, checked by whom. Aviation law shows it can be done; the other sectors mostly gesture at it.

Make the safety knowledge public

When a law points to a standard, the standard becomes law in effect. Fifty-seven of our sixty-one standards are behind a paywall. Rules people cannot read are rules people cannot follow.

What we do not know

The honest edges of the map

74 official sources refused our crawler, mostly France, Norway, Korea, Taiwan and Chinese provinces. They are listed for a person with a browser. Our crawler identifies itself honestly and does not pretend to be one.

Old statutes hide their robot provisions. A road-traffic act from 1960 can carry a 2023 article on automated driving; we could date the provision in only 28 of the 278 rules. Trend lines on binding law are therefore floors, not histories.

English is 74% of the collection. Chinese, Japanese, Spanish and Portuguese texts were read in the original, but the search was richest in English and in the EU.

No human has labelled a document. The next step in this study is a person, not a model.

To remember

Five things to take home

1

Robots are governed sideways. 278 binding rules reach them; 25 name AI. They are aviation, road, machinery and product law.

2

The robots we will live with have almost none. Companions 0, humanoids 0, care robots 4, among 429 documents about them.

3

The body changes the rulebook. Physical safety, certification, accountability and liability lead; transparency, fairness and dignity barely appear.

4

Mention is not detail. Six rules in ten raise the control question; fewer than three in ten answer it. Duties fall on operators, rarely on the people who write the software.

5

Every count depends on where you looked. Ours went 5, 299, 195, 278. Ask any number about AI regulation how it was made.

Thank you

The question is not whether robots will come into our homes. It is whether the rules will be there when they do.
